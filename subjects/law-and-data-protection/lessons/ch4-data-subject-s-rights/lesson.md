# Learning Objectives

Master Chapter 4 — Data subject's rights — well enough to answer both multiple-choice and written exam questions on it.

# Lesson

> **In one sentence:** The GDPR gives every person a toolbox to control their data: you can find out what is held, get a copy, fix it, delete it, pause it, take it elsewhere, say "stop", and refuse to be judged only by a machine.

## The rights at a glance

| Right                                   | GDPR article    | One-line meaning                                                             |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| To be informed                          | Arts. 13 and 14 | Be told clearly what happens to your data                                    |
| Of access                               | Art. 15         | Confirmation, a copy, and supplementary information                          |
| To rectification                        | Art. 16         | Correct inaccurate data, complete incomplete data                            |
| To erasure ("right to be forgotten")    | Art. 17         | Have data deleted in listed situations                                       |
| To restriction                          | Art. 18         | Temporarily freeze processing                                                |
| To data portability                     | Art. 20         | Receive data in machine-readable form or move it                             |
| To object                               | Art. 21         | Stop processing based on public task, legitimate interests, direct marketing |
| Automated decision-making and profiling | Art. 22         | Protection from decisions made solely by machines                            |

> **🧠 Memory hook**
>
> "**I** **A**m **R**eally **E**xcited, **R**ights **P**rotect **O**ur **A**utonomy": Informed, Access, Rectification, Erasure, Restriction, Portability, Object, Automated decisions.

## Right to be informed

The controller must give clear and concise information about what it does with data. It must be **concise, transparent, intelligible, easily accessible, in clear and plain language**; given in **writing** (electronically where appropriate) or **orally** on request if identity is proven; **without excessive delay or expense**.

### Content `Art. 13(1)`

- Controller identity and contact details, and the DPO's details if any
- Purpose and legal basis; the legitimate interest if that is the basis
- Recipients or categories of recipients
- Transfers to third countries or international organisations, and whether based on adequacy or safeguards
- Storage period or the criteria to decide it
- Data subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection)
- Whether provision is required by law or contract, whether the person must provide it, and consequences of not providing it
- Existence of automated decision-making and profiling, with meaningful information on **the logic, its significance and envisaged consequences**
- Right to complain to a supervisory authority; right to withdraw consent
- If data was not obtained from the person: its **origin**

If the controller wants to use data for a **new purpose**, it must inform the person **before** the further processing. If consent was the basis, **renewed consent** is needed when the purpose changes or new purposes are added.

### Timing

| Data source                              | When to inform                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Directly from the data subject `Art. 13` | At the time the data are obtained                                                                        |
| From other sources `Art. 14`             | Within a reasonable period, **at the latest within one month**, or before disclosure to a third party |

### Methods

**Layered** notices (short notice plus detailed layers), **dashboards** (preference management), **just-in-time** notices (at the moment of collection), **icons**, and **mobile or smart device** features (pop-ups, voice alerts, gestures).

The controller must also inform people of the **right to lodge a complaint** with a supervisory authority and, if necessary, a national court `Art. 13(2)(d)`.

### Exemptions from the duty to inform

- The person already has the information.
- For data not obtained from the person: providing it is **impossible or disproportionate** (e.g. archiving, research, statistics).
- A necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society: national and public security, defence, judicial investigations, economic and financial interests, or more compelling private interests.

## Right of access

"Subject access" gives the right to **confirmation** whether one's data is processed, a **copy** of the data, and **supplementary information**. It lets people understand how and why data is used and check it is lawful.

## Right to rectification

Compel the controller to **correct inaccurate data without undue delay**, and to **complete incomplete data** (including by a supplementary statement). Data is inaccurate if **incorrect or misleading as to any matter of fact**. Example: a GP diagnoses an illness that later proves wrong.

- For **legally significant matters** (legal identity, address for legal documents) the controller may demand **proof** of the inaccuracy.
- While accuracy is being checked, processing should be **restricted**, whether or not the person formally asked for restriction.

> **⚖️ ECtHR cases on inaccurate data (Art. 8 ECHR violated)**
>
> **Cemalettin Canli v Turkey**: a police report wrongly described the applicant as a member of illegal organisations, though he was never convicted. Refusing to amend it violated Art. 8.
**Ciubotaru v Moldova**: the applicant could not change his registered ethnicity from Moldovan to Romanian despite objective evidence; the State failed its positive obligation. Violation of Art. 8.

### Refusing a request: manifestly unfounded or excessive

| Manifestly unfounded                                                                                                                                                                                              | Excessive                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| No real intention to exercise the right (offers to withdraw in return for a benefit); malicious, to harass or disrupt; unsubstantiated; targets a particular employee; systematic campaign (e.g. weekly requests) | Repeats the substance of previous requests, or overlaps with other requests |

## Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten") `Art. 17`

Data must be erased **without undue delay** where:

- It is **no longer necessary** for the purposes.
- The person **withdraws consent** and there is no other legal ground.
- The person **objects** and there are no overriding legitimate grounds.
- It was **unlawfully processed**.
- Erasure is required by a **legal obligation** in EU or Member State law.
- It was collected in relation to **information society services offered to children** `Art. 8`.

**Exceptions**: freedom of expression and information; compliance with a legal obligation; public task or official authority; public health; archiving, research or statistics; establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims. A request may also be refused if manifestly unfounded or excessive.

If the data was **made public**, the controller must take **reasonable steps**, considering available technology and cost, to inform other controllers processing it about the erasure request.

> **⚖️ Cases**
>
> **Google Spain** (CJEU, C-131/12, 2014): a Spanish man wanted outdated newspaper reports of his debts removed from Google results. Google argued it only links to others' pages. The CJEU held that a search engine, when indexing and ranking content, **is a controller**. Search results can build a detailed profile, so a fair balance is needed between internet users' interest in information and the person's rights under Arts. 7 and 8 of the Charter.
**Brunet v France** (ECtHR): details of a man whose criminal proceedings were discontinued stayed in a police database for 20 years with no way to delete them. Violation of Art. 8.

## Right to restriction of processing `Art. 18`

Temporarily stops the controller from processing. Grounds:

- Accuracy is **contested** (while it is checked).
- Processing is **unlawful** but the person prefers restriction to erasure.
- The person needs the data kept for **legal claims**.
- An **objection is pending** while the controller's legitimate grounds are weighed.

Methods: temporarily moving data to another system, making it unavailable to users, or temporarily removing it. Restricted data may only be **stored**, unless: the person consents; for legal claims; to protect another person's rights; or for important public interest. The controller must **notify the person before lifting** the restriction and communicate rectification, erasure or restriction to each recipient unless impossible or disproportionate.

## Right to data portability `Art. 20`

Receive data **you provided** in a **structured, commonly used and machine-readable format**, and have it transmitted **directly to another controller where technically feasible**. Example: exporting your contact list from webmail to build a wedding list.

Applies **only** when the lawful basis is **consent or contract** AND processing is carried out by **automated means** (not paper files). Controllers should develop **interoperable** formats (systems able to exchange data), but the GDPR does not prescribe a specific format, so formats differ between sectors.

## Right to object `Art. 21`

- **General objection**: on grounds relating to the person's particular situation, where the basis is **public task or legitimate interests**, including profiling. After a successful objection the controller must stop; processing done **before** the objection remains lawful.
- **Direct marketing**: object **at any time and free of charge**; people must be told about this right clearly and **separately** from other information.
- **By automated means** for **information society services** (services normally provided for remuneration, at a distance, by electronic means, at the individual request of the recipient). Controllers need technical arrangements, e.g. blocking cookies or turning off tracking.
- **Research and statistics**: people can object unless processing is necessary for a task in the public interest. Scientific research is interpreted broadly (technological development, fundamental, applied, privately funded); historical research includes genealogy. Note: the right to erasure does **not** apply where processing is necessary for research or statistics.

## Automated decision-making and profiling `Art. 22`

Decisions made **solely by automated means, without human intervention**, e.g. an online loan decision, or a recruitment aptitude test with pre-programmed criteria. It does not have to involve profiling, though it often does.

Decisions with legal or similarly significant effects are acceptable only if: **necessary for a contract**; based on **explicit consent**; or **authorised by law** with safeguards for the person's rights. People must be told it exists, with meaningful information on the **logic involved** and the **envisaged consequences**. Example: a health insurer should explain how its algorithm works and which factors set the premium.

> **⚠️ Exam traps**
>
> - Portability is narrower than access: only data the person **provided**, only for **consent or contract**, only **automated** processing.
>
> - Objecting to **direct marketing** is absolute; a general objection can be overridden by compelling legitimate grounds.
>
> - Restriction is a **pause**; erasure is **deletion**. You can ask for restriction instead of erasure if processing is unlawful.
>
> - Indirectly obtained data: inform within **one month** at the latest.
