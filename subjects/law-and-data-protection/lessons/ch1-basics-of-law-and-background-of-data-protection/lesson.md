# Lesson

> **In one sentence:** Law is the set of official, enforceable rules made by the state. Data protection law is the part of law that keeps information about people safe and under their control, and it grew out of the human right to privacy.

## What is law?

A **system of rules and principles established by governmental authority to regulate behaviour within society.**

| Key characteristic   | Meaning                                         |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Binding nature       | Creates enforceable obligations and rights      |
| Systematic structure | An organised body of rules, not random commands |
| Social function      | Addresses collective needs and maintains order  |
| Normative framework  | Sets standards for acceptable conduct           |

## How law differs from other rules (morality, religion, custom)

| Criterion            | Law                                                   | Other rules                                      |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Source and authority | Official government bodies with power to make rules   | Tradition, religion, social consensus            |
| Enforcement          | Formal sanctions: fines, imprisonment, civil remedies | Social pressure, morality, informal consequences |
| Scope                | Applies universally to all people in a country        | Group-specific or voluntary                      |
| Formal recognition   | Officially codified and documented                    | Customary or unwritten                           |

## How are legal rules created?

- **Legislative process**: statutes enacted by elected bodies (parliaments, congresses); regulations issued by administrative agencies.
- **Judicial development**: case law through court decisions and precedent; common law principles developed over time.
- **International sources**: domestic rules adapted from international treaties and agreements, and from customary international law.

## The five roles of law

- **Regulatory**: setting standards and prohibiting harmful conduct.
- **Protective**: safeguarding rights and protecting vulnerable groups.
- **Facilitative**: enabling transactions and economic activity.
- **Dispute resolution**: peaceful conflict resolution mechanisms.
- **Educational**: communicating and shaping societal values.

> **🧠 Memory hook**
>
> "**R**eal **P**eople **F**ollow **D**ecent **E**xamples": Regulatory, Protective, Facilitative, Dispute resolution, Educational.

## Why do we obey the law?

- Fear of punishment and sanctions.
- **Social contract**: people give up some personal freedoms in exchange for the protection and order that laws provide.
- Mutual benefit from an organised society.
- **Legitimacy**: recognition of the government's right to rule.
- Moral duty to follow just laws (e.g. from religious teachings).
- **Socialisation**: we learn from a young age to respect authority.

## Data protection and the right to privacy

**Data protection** = safeguarding personal and sensitive information from unauthorised access, use, disclosure or destruction. (The lecture notes it is also called "data security".)

**The right to privacy** classically means: the right to be left alone; the right against undue government intrusion into personal issues and decisions; protection of one's personal information from public scrutiny. In practice it includes deciding who can access your data, freedom from surveillance, controlling how data is collected, stored and used, secure personal spaces, making personal choices without interference, and protecting online communications.

Personal data is part of what privacy protects. Privacy rights over data include: deciding what is gathered, demanding consent, restricting processing and sharing, knowing what data exists about you, having data removed, controlling where it goes, limiting systematic monitoring, requiring de-identification, and challenging improper handling.

## Why data protection is important: six reasons

| Reason                        | Scenario from the lecture                                               |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Protecting individual privacy | A customer database leak exposes addresses, phone numbers, card details |
| Enhancing customer trust      | A customer shares data comfortably because it is handled securely       |
| Mitigating financial risks    | A breach causes losses through unauthorised access to financial data    |
| Enhancing business reputation | A privacy scandal damages reputation and loses customers                |
| Preventing cyber threats      | Weak protection leads to a ransomware attack                            |
| Ensuring legal compliance     | Failing GDPR or CCPA brings heavy fines                                 |

Data protection interacts with four fields: **law** (a framework for accountability and rights), **human rights** (privacy, freedom of expression, non-discrimination), **business** (trust, risk, competitive advantage) and **technology** (more data collection means ethical, responsible use is needed).

## How data protection law developed

Computers and the information society created new risks to private life, which created the need for rules on collecting and using personal information. New concepts appeared: **"informational privacy"** and the **"right to informational self-determination"**. EU data protection began in the **1970s**, when some states adopted laws to control processing by public authorities and large companies.

| Year        | Instrument                                                              | Why it matters                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1948        | Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)                            | `Art. 12` respect for private and family life                                                                                                    |
| 1950 / 1953 | European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), adopted 1950, in force 1953 | `Art. 8` right to respect for private life, which includes personal data protection. European Court of Human Rights set up in Strasbourg in 1959 |
| 1966        | International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)            | No arbitrary or unlawful interference with privacy, home, correspondence; no unlawful attacks on honour and reputation                           |
| 1980        | Council of Europe **Convention 108**                                 | Protection of individuals with regard to automatic processing of personal data. Committee of Ministers also adopted non-binding recommendations  |
| 1995        | Directive 95/46/EC (24 Oct 1995), the Data Protection Directive         | Main EU instrument from 1995 until May 2018                                                                                                      |
| 2000 / 2009 | Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU                                 | Adopted 2000, legally binding EU primary law from **1 December 2009**. `Art. 8` right to personal data protection                             |
| 2013 onward | UN resolutions "The Right to Privacy in the Digital Age"                | UN attention to digital privacy                                                                                                                  |
| 2016 / 2018 | General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)                               | Adopted April 2016, fully applicable **25 May 2018**. Model for Chile, Japan, Brazil, South Korea, Argentina, Kenya                           |

> **⚠️ Exam traps**
>
> - The **UN framework does NOT recognise personal data protection as a fundamental right**. It protects privacy (UDHR Art. 12, ICCPR).
>
> - The original treaties of the European Communities contained **no reference to human rights**. Human rights came later through the Charter.
>
> - The ECHR (Council of Europe, Strasbourg court) is not the same as the EU Charter (EU law, Luxembourg court, the CJEU).
>
> - The GDPR replaced the Directive because the 1995 Directive was **ineffective** (the slide's phrase "1995 GDPR" refers to the Directive).
