# Learning Objectives

Master Chapter 3 — Core principles of the GDPR — well enough to answer both multiple-choice and written exam questions on it.

# Lesson

> **In one sentence:** Before you touch anyone's data you need a lawful reason, you must be fair and open, use it only for the stated purpose, take only what you need, keep it correct, delete it when done, lock it up safely, and be able to prove you did all of this.

## The seven principles `Art. 5`

- Lawfulness, fairness and transparency `5(1)(a)`
- Purpose limitation `5(1)(b)`
- Data minimisation `5(1)&lpar;c&rpar;`
- Accuracy `5(1)(d)`
- Storage limitation `5(1)(e)`
- Integrity and confidentiality (security) `5(1)(f)`
- Accountability `5(2)`

> **🧠 Memory hook**
>
> "**L**awyers **P**rotect **M**y **A**wesome **S**ecret **I**nformation **A**lways": Lawfulness, Purpose, Minimisation, Accuracy, Storage, Integrity, Accountability.

**Why they matter:** compliance is the building block of good practice, and infringing the basic principles is in the **highest tier of fines** `Art. 83(5)(a)`: up to **€20 million or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher**.

## 1. Lawfulness, fairness and transparency

"Personal data shall be processed lawfully, fairly and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject." Detailed rules on lawful basis are in **Arts. 6 to 10**; transparency details in **Arts. 13 and 14** (right to be informed). The three elements overlap and **all three must be satisfied**. The organisation must specify why it needs the data and for what purpose, and the data cannot be used against the person.

### Lawfulness: the six lawful bases `Art. 6(1)`

At least **one** must apply. Extra conditions apply to sensitive data.

| Basis                    | Meaning                                                                                                                   | Lecture example                                                              |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| (a) Consent              | Clear consent for a specific purpose                                                                                      | Newsletter sign-up                                                           |
| (b) Contract             | Necessary to perform a contract with the data subject, or pre-contract steps at their request                             | Online shop processes card details; shares name and address with the courier |
| &lpar;c&rpar; Legal obligation     | Necessary to comply with a law the controller is subject to                                                               | Payroll reports salaries to the tax authority; keeps records 7 years         |
| (d) Vital interests      | Necessary to protect someone's life. Reserved for genuine emergencies                                                     | Unconscious man; paramedics find his penicillin-allergy card                 |
| (e) Public task          | Task in the public interest or official authority, **established by law**, processing necessary; usually public bodies | Department of Motor Vehicles manages licences under the Motor Vehicles Act   |
| (f) Legitimate interests | Necessary for legitimate interests of controller or third party (balanced against the individual)                         | CCTV in office common areas to prevent theft and keep staff safe             |

> **🧠 Memory hook for the six bases**
>
> "**C**ool **C**ats **L**ike **V**ietnamese **P**ho for **L**unch": Consent, Contract, Legal obligation, Vital interests, Public task, Legitimate interests.

### Consent in detail

Consent = "any **freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous** indication of the data subject's wishes by which he or she, by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, signifies agreement". Processing is permitted if expressly allowed by law **or** if the data subject consents.

- **Freely given**: voluntary, a real choice. Inappropriate pressure or influence invalidates consent.
- **Specific**: tells the controller's identity, what data, how it will be used, and the purpose. Must refer to clearly identified processing activities.
- **Informed**: includes the **right to withdraw at any time** (as easy as giving it), use for automated decision-making, and risks of transfers without an adequacy decision or safeguards.
- **Unambiguous**: a statement or clear affirmative act (opt-in, declaration, active motion). No specific form required, but **silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity are NOT consent**; consent cannot be implied.

Consent can be part of terms of service, but the request must be in **clear and plain language**, intelligible and easily accessible. Any part of the declaration that violates the GDPR is **not binding**.

> **⚖️ Example: bundled consent fails "specific"**
>
> An online shop's checkout asks users to "consent to the use of your data for marketing, the transfer to non-EU providers and the sharing of data with partners". One consent for three purposes fails the specific element. Just naming "partners" is not specific if the recipients are known.

### When is processing still unlawful?

- **Using unlawfully obtained data**: a marketing firm buys 100,000 contacts that a broker hacked from a competitor's CRM. A legitimate business purpose does not cure illegal data.
- **Expired consent, violated retention, new purpose**: a fitness app keeps health data years past its 2-year deletion promise and sells it to a pharmaceutical company.
- **Processing that commits a criminal offence**: a fake loan website harvests IDs and bank details for identity fraud; a hacker uses stolen dating-site data for extortion.
- Processing that results in: a **breach of a duty of confidence** (Dr Smith tells a neighbour a patient has HIV); **exceeding legal powers** (Officer James uses public cameras to track his ex-girlfriend); **copyright infringement** (EduTech emails a scanned copyrighted book to 500 students); **breach of contract** (IT Solutions keeps a bank's real customer data for demos); **breach of industry regulation** (MobileTel keeps call logs 5 years when the limit is 6 months); **breach of the ECHR** (a law allowing blanket surveillance of every citizen without court order).

If data is processed unlawfully, individuals have the right to have it **erased** or its processing **restricted**.

### Fairness: three elements

| Element                                                                                                  | Fair                                                                                   | Unfair                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reasonable expectations**: handle data as people would reasonably expect, no surprises or deception | Food delivery app uses location to show nearby restaurants                             | Flashlight app sells your contact list; recruitment site shares CVs with insurers                              |
| **No unjustified adverse effects**: some effects can be justified if proportionate                    | Bank credit check before a loan; criminal record check for jobs with vulnerable people | Insurer denies cover from social media posts with no transparent criteria; landlord discriminates by ethnicity |
| **Informing of risks**: proactively tell people about risks                                           | Health app warns anonymised data carries small re-identification risk                  | App records microphone continuously without clear warning                                                      |

### Transparency

Being **clear, open and honest** about who you are, how and why you use personal data, explained in an easily accessible, easy-to-understand way. Information must be given **before** processing and remain accessible **during** processing; data subjects are also notified about access requests to their own data.

## 2. Purpose limitation `Art. 5(1)(b)`

Collected for **specified, explicit and legitimate purposes** and not further processed in a manner **incompatible** with those purposes. The purpose must be defined **before** processing starts. Further processing for **archiving in the public interest, scientific or historical research, or statistics** is not considered incompatible `Art. 89(1)`.

A new purpose is acceptable if: it is **compatible** with the original; or you get **specific consent** for it; or a **clear legal provision** requires or allows it in the public interest.

**Compatibility factors:** the link between the purposes; the context of collection and reasonable expectations; the nature of the data (is it sensitive?); the possible consequences; appropriate safeguards (encryption, pseudonymisation). A new purpose that is very different, unexpected or has unjustified impact is likely incompatible.

> **⚖️ Example**
>
> A GP gives his patient list to his wife's travel agency so she can offer recuperation holidays. This is **incompatible** with the purpose for which the data was obtained (medical care).

## 3. Data minimisation `Art. 5(1)&lpar;c&rpar;`

Personal data shall be **adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary** in relation to the purposes. Processing must be limited to what is necessary for a legitimate purpose, happen only when the purpose cannot reasonably be achieved by other means, and not disproportionately interfere with rights.

- **Too much**: more than needed, or irrelevant details. A debt collector gathering data on several people with a similar name must delete most of it, keeping only a minimal record so they are not contacted again. Do not collect "on the off-chance" it might be useful; but holding data for a foreseeable event is fine if justified: an employer may hold blood groups of **hazardous-work** employees, but holding them for the whole workforce is excessive.
- **Inadequate**: insufficient for the purpose. A small club managed with names and emails grows large and now needs more data to identify members and track payments.

> **⚖️ Cases**
>
> **Digital Rights Ireland** (CJEU, C-293/12 and C-594/12, 8 April 2014): the Data Retention Directive covered all individuals, all means of electronic communication and all traffic data without differentiation, limitation or exception. Fighting serious crime is a genuine general interest, but the blanket approach was problematic.
**Transport chip card**: a town council's card logs every trip against a database of names. Minimisation would only require the reader to confirm the card is valid, without recording who travelled when.

> **⚠️ Classic exam question: purpose limitation vs data minimisation**
>
> Purpose limitation asks **WHY**: use data only for the stated purpose and compatible ones. Data minimisation asks **HOW MUCH**: collect only the data needed for that purpose. A shop that collects your date of birth "for delivery" breaks minimisation; a shop that uses your delivery address for political mailing breaks purpose limitation.

## 4. Accuracy `Art. 5(1)(d)`

Accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; every reasonable step must be taken to **erase or rectify inaccurate data without delay**. Inaccurate means **"incorrect or misleading as to any matter of fact"**. The duty depends on the **purpose** of processing.

- Sometimes updating is **prohibited**: records that document events as a **historical snapshot**. A medical record of an operation must not be changed even if findings turn out wrong; only additions clearly marked as later contributions.
- Sometimes regular checking is an **absolute necessity**: credit databases, where outdated data can harm people applying for loans.

> **⚖️ Rijkeboer**
>
> (CJEU, C-553/07, 7 May 2009)A Dutch man asked his local authority which recipients had received his records over the previous two years, and what data was disclosed. The CJEU held that the right to privacy means being certain data is processed correctly and lawfully, and that the **right of access** exists so people can check their data is accurate.

## 5. Storage limitation `Art. 5(1)(e)`

Kept in a form that permits identification for **no longer than necessary**. Longer storage is allowed **solely** for archiving in the public interest, scientific or historical research or statistics, with appropriate technical and organisational safeguards `Art. 89(1)`.

**Exceptions** are allowed only if **provided by law**, **respect the essence** of fundamental rights, and are **necessary and proportionate** for legitimate aims: national security, investigating and prosecuting crime, carrying out criminal penalties, protecting the data subject and the rights of others.

> **⚖️ Cases**
>
> **S. and Marper v UK** (ECtHR): indefinite retention of fingerprints, cell samples and DNA of two people who were acquitted or whose case was discontinued was disproportionate and unnecessary in a democratic society.
**Digital Rights Ireland**: the Directive required retention of 6 to 24 months without objective criteria to limit the period to what is strictly necessary.

## 6. Integrity and confidentiality (security) `Art. 5(1)(f)`

Processed with **appropriate security**, including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate technical or organisational measures. It covers cybersecurity and also physical and organisational security.

`Art. 32(1)` Measures must take into account: **the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context and purposes of processing, and the risk** (likelihood and severity) to people's rights. **Pseudonymisation and encryption** are named examples.

Technical areas: **system** security, **data** security (access controls), **online** security (websites, apps), **device** security (including Bring-Your-Own-Device policies).

Possible harms from poor security: identity fraud, fake card transactions, targeting by fraudsters, witnesses put at risk, offenders at risk from vigilantes, exposure of addresses of police, prison officers and domestic violence victims, fake tax-credit applications, mortgage fraud.

## 7. Accountability `Art. 5(2)`

"The controller shall be **responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance with**" the other principles. `Art. 24(1)` Controllers implement risk-based, proportionate technical and organisational measures, and review and update them.

- **Controllers** must be able to demonstrate compliance to data subjects, the public and supervisory authorities at any time.
- **Processors** must comply with obligations linked to accountability, such as keeping records of processing and appointing a Data Protection Officer.

> **⚠️ Exam traps**
>
> - Accountability has two parts: being compliant **and proving it**. Doing the right thing without documentation is not enough.
>
> - Vital interests is only for real emergencies, not for convenience.
>
> - Public task must be **set by law**, not just decided by the organisation.
>
> - A legitimate business purpose does not make processing lawful if the data itself was obtained illegally.
